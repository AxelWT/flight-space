# Is the "Piece of Cake" Mentality After Big Games Good or Bad?

Simply put, this mindset is a double-edged sword. At certain moments, it's your "calming pill," but more often than not, it can be a dangerous "sweet trap."

Let's break down this mentality and see what effects it actually has on you:

## 🛡️ Upside: It's the Ballast for Your Stress Resistance

If you've truly experienced high-pressure situations, then when facing relatively lower-level competitions, this "big picture" perspective can help you effectively reduce anxiety.

- **Dispels stage fright**: You've seen bigger stages, so having more spectators or a tighter score won't make your palms sweat or your heart race.
- **Maintains emotional stability**: This confidence allows you to appear calm on the field. Even if you fall behind by a few points, you feel "this is within my experience range" and can more calmly organize a comeback.

At this level, it's a good thing — it shows you have a strong foundation of self-confidence.

## ⚠️ Downside: It Easily Turns Into Fatal Complacency

However, sports psychology tells us that once this "piece of cake" thought takes over, subtle changes happen in your subconscious. This state is called **"pre-competition overconfidence"** in sports psychology.

It quietly brings several fatal problems:

### 1. A Cliff-like Drop in Attention

When you think the opponent is "no match for you," your focus decreases. You might miss the opponent's movements, ignore score changes, or even zone out during serves.

### 2. Slack Execution

You unconsciously feel you can "win just by playing casually," leading to inadequate warm-ups, lazy defensive footwork, and careless shot selection. In competitive sports, losing defensive focus is often the beginning of a collapse.

### 3. Poor Resilience (Bubble Burst Effect)

This is the scariest part. If you enter the game expecting to "dominate," once the opponent strikes back or you make early mistakes, your mental defense collapses instantly. The huge psychological gap from "I can easily win" to "Why can't I beat them" quickly destroys morale and even causes your technique to fall apart.

## 💡 What's a Better Mindset?

True masters never use past glory to look down on their current opponent. If you want to perform at your best, try adjusting to these three mindsets:

### 1. "Prepare as if You've Never Won"

As Olympic champion Eileen Gu said: "Train as if you've never won; compete as if you've never lost."

No matter how many championships you've won in the past, clear your record when you step onto the field. Imagine your opponent as a brand-new challenger worthy of your full effort.

### 2. Extreme Focus on "This One Play"

Don't think about "this game is easy for me." Instead, focus on the current technical movement: stabilize your center of gravity, read the opponent's movements, make decisive moves. Break complex games down into simple actions and enter your own **Flow State**.

### 3. "Respect and Ruthlessness" in Balance

Respect your opponent — treat every game like a final. Be ruthless — don't let emotions sway you, never let up no matter how far ahead you are, and never panic no matter how far behind.

## Summary

Your past "big stage" experiences should be the foundation of your courage to win, not an excuse to look down on the present. True masters never say "this game is a piece of cake." Instead, they roll up their sleeves and say: "No matter what game it is, I'll give my all to this one play!"
