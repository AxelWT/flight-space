# Counseling for Toad — Robert de Board

> Book Notes

---

## About the Author

**Robert de Board (1932–2020)**, British psychologist, graduated from Selwyn College, Cambridge, and later served at Henley Business School teaching organizational behavior and psychology.

He was a practitioner and promoter of **Transactional Analysis (TA)**. This theory, founded by Eric Berne in the 1950s, is a personality theory centered on "ego states." De Board skillfully wove TA theory into the framework of *The Wind in the Willows*, letting Toad gradually emerge from depression through 10 counseling sessions, making complex psychological concepts warm and accessible.

Since its publication in 1997, the book has been translated into more than ten languages and sold millions of copies worldwide, becoming a classic introduction to psychological counseling.

---

## Story Background

The story uses characters from Kenneth Grahame's *The Wind in the Willows* — the once cheerful and flamboyant Toad falls into severe depression. His friends (Rat, Mole, Badger) persuade him to see a counselor named Heron. Over 10 sessions, Toad gradually understands the source of his emotions and learns to take responsibility for his own life.

## Core Theory: Three Ego States

This is the most central framework of the book. Transactional Analysis holds that everyone has three ego states within them simultaneously:

### Child Ego State

When you're in the Child state, your feelings and behaviors regress to childhood patterns.

| Type | Characteristics | Manifestations |
|------|----------------|----------------|
| Natural Child | Innate basic emotions | Joy, anger, sadness, fear — life energy itself |
| Adapted Child | Adjusted in response to parental demands | Compliance, pleasing, apologizing, sulking, tantrums, dependence |
| Rebellious Child | Resistance to parental control | Procrastination, half-heartedness, deliberate defiance, passive resistance |

> The root of Toad's problem: He has been stuck in the "Adapted Child" state for a long time — habitually pleasing others, constantly apologizing, afraid to express anger. This stems from his harsh, critical father and weak, submissive mother.

### Parent Ego State

When you're in the Parent state, you're repeating values and behavior patterns learned from your parents.

| Type | Characteristics | Manifestations |
|------|----------------|----------------|
| Critical Parent | Criticism, punishment, control | "Why are you always like this?" "You should be ashamed" |
| Nurturing Parent | Care, protection, support | "It's for your own good" "Let me help you" |

- The Parent state isn't just directed at others — **you can also play the Critical Parent toward yourself**, engaging in constant self-criticism and self-punishment.
- Badger is the classic Critical Parent, always criticizing Toad.
- Key insight: **Self-criticism is often harsher than external criticism.**

### Adult Ego State

When you're in the Adult state, you think rationally about present facts rather than being driven by past emotions.

- The Adult state is the only state where you can **learn new things** and **make changes**.
- In the Child state, you can only re-experience old emotions; in the Parent state, you only repeat old doctrines.
- Only by entering the Adult state can you truly analyze your situation and take action.
- **No one can "make" you enter the Adult state — it's your choice.**

> Heron the counselor repeatedly emphasizes: Change only happens when you are willing to take responsibility for yourself.

## Core Theory: Life Positions

During childhood, everyone forms a set of basic beliefs about whether "I'm OK" and whether "Others are OK." This is called a **Life Position**. It determines your lifelong behavior patterns and psychological games.

| Life Position | Meaning | Typical Behavior |
|--------------|---------|-----------------|
| **I'm OK, You're OK** | Healthy position | Confident and trusting, willing to cooperate and grow |
| **I'm OK, You're Not OK** | Arrogant/Paranoid | Always right, others always wrong, tends to blame others |
| **I'm Not OK, You're OK** | Depressed/Inferior | Always feels it's their fault, others are better, people-pleasing |
| **I'm Not OK, You're Not OK** | Desperate/Nihilistic | Loses faith in both self and others, most dangerous state |

> Toad's starting point was "I'm Not OK, You're OK" — he habitually belittled himself and looked up to others. He played the victim role his whole life.

### Key Insights

- Whichever position you choose, you will unconsciously **collect evidence to prove it right** — this is called "confirmation bias" in psychological games.
- "I'm OK, You're OK" isn't natural — it's a **conscious choice of belief** — you need to actively believe in yourself and others.
- Once a life position is set in childhood, it constantly self-validates in adulthood — unless you become aware of it and actively change.

## Core Theory: Psychological Games

Psychological games are a key concept in Transactional Analysis. They refer to unconscious, repetitive interaction patterns aimed at **validating one's life position**.

### Common Psychological Games

| Game Name | Position | How It Works |
|-----------|---------|-------------|
| **Poor Me** | I'm Not OK, You're OK | Constantly displaying misfortune to gain sympathy without seeking change |
| **See What You Made Me Do** | I'm Not OK, You're OK | Blaming others for one's own behavior — "You made me do it" |
| **Now I've Got You** | I'm OK, You're Not OK | Waiting for others to make mistakes, then attacking fiercely |
| **Yes, But** | I'm Not OK, You're OK | Superficially accepting advice while finding reasons to reject it — "Yes, but... I can't" |

> Toad's most common games were "Poor Me" and "Yes, But" — outwardly seeking help, but actually proving "I can't change."

### Characteristics of Psychological Games

- Games are unconscious — the player doesn't know they're "playing"
- Game results always validate your life position (self-fulfilling prophecy)
- Games provide **negative stroking** — painful, but with a distorted sense of "security"
- To break out of a game, you must first recognize it — **awareness is the beginning of change**

## The Counseling Process: Key Progress in 10 Sessions

| Session | Topic | Key Progress |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| 1 | Starting counseling | Toad reluctantly arrives; Heron makes him understand: **counseling must be voluntary**, not for someone else |
| 2 | Feeling emotions | Heron guides Toad to identify and name his emotions — he realizes he's been suppressing anger |
| 3 | Child Ego State | Toad first understands how childhood experiences shaped his current behavior patterns |
| 4 | Exploring childhood | Deep reflection on his relationship with his parents — a harsh father and weak mother shaped his Adapted Child |
| 5 | Expressing anger | Toad realizes he never learned to express anger properly — either suppressing it or throwing tantrums |
| 6 | Parent Ego State | Understands Badger's Critical Parent behavior, and also sees his own internal self-criticism |
| 7 | Adult Ego State | Toad experiences responding with rationality rather than emotion for the first time, feeling empowered |
| 8 | Life Positions | Recognizes his long-standing "I'm Not OK, You're OK" position and begins to understand it's a choice |
| 9 | Psychological Games | Identifies the games he commonly plays and decides to stop being a victim |
| 10 | Moving toward "I'm OK, You're OK" | Toad chooses a new life position, makes plans for the future; counseling ends |

## Core Insights

### Take Responsibility for Yourself

> **No one can "make" you feel anything unless you agree to it.**

- We choose our feelings — anger, sadness, victimhood are all our own choices
- "He made me angry" — No, you chose to respond with anger
- Admitting this means leaving the comfort zone of victimhood, but it also brings true freedom
- This isn't blaming the victim — it's helping Toad (and the reader) realize they have **the power to choose**

### The Many Faces of Anger

Many people think anger only comes in one form — "explosion" — but hidden anger is equally destructive:

- **Sulking**: Punishing others with silence
- **Tantrums**: Using loss of control to manipulate
- **Delaying**: Expressing dissatisfaction through procrastination
- **Cold shoulder**: Inflicting harm through neglect

All of these come from the Adapted Child state — substitute ways of "not daring to express anger directly."

### True Listening

Heron as a counselor demonstrates:

- Doesn't give advice, but uses questions to guide Toad to find answers himself
- Responds consistently — words align with actions, doesn't play psychological games
- When Toad tries the "Poor Me" game for sympathy, Heron doesn't play along but guides him back to the Adult state
- **True help isn't solving someone's problems, but helping them find their own answers**

### The Courage to Change

- Change means giving up old securities — even painful ones
- Part of the reason Toad stayed depressed was that the "victim" identity let him off the hook from responsibility
- Moving from "I'm Not OK" to "I'm OK" takes courage — you can no longer blame your parents, environment, or others
- **The essence of growth is shifting from "passive endurance" to "active choice"**
